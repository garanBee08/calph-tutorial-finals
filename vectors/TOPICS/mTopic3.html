Accuracy, Precision, and Errors

In physics, when we take measurements, we need to know how reliable and consistent they are. Understanding accuracy, precision, and errors helps us evaluate the quality of our measurements and improve our experimental techniques. These concepts are fundamental to scientific investigation and ensure that our results are meaningful and trustworthy.

Difference of Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy

How close you are to the true value. An accurate measurement has a very small error — it is very close to the actual or correct value.

Precision

How consistent your measurements are (or whether it all starts to overlap). High precision means your measurements are close together, even if they're not close to the true value.

Think of a dartboard: If your darts hit near the bullseye, that's accurate. If your darts cluster together (even if far from the bullseye), that's precise. Ideally, you want both — darts clustered tightly around the bullseye!

Error

An error is the difference between a measured value and the true accepted value of a quantity. It shows how much your measurement deviates from what it should be. It is not equal to "mistake" because here in physics, an error isn't just a mistake — it's a way to measure the quality of a measurement.
Formula for Error: Error = Measured Value − True Value

Example: If the true length of an object is 10.0 cm, but you measure it as 9.8 cm, the error is: 9.8 cm − 10.0 cm = −0.2 cm

Types of Errors

Systematic Error

These are consistent errors that happen in the same direction every time. This can be either too high or too low.

  • ▸ Always shifts results in the same direction (too high or too low)
  • ▸ Often caused by faulty instruments or incorrect calibration
  • ▸ Affects accuracy but not precision
Random Error

These errors vary unpredictably from one measurement to another. They occur due to uncontrollable or unknown factors, such as small environmental changes (such as air currents, vibrations, human reaction time).

  • ▸ Vary in size and direction
  • ▸ Affects precision but not accuracy
  • ▸ Can be reduced by taking multiple measurements and averaging them
Gross Error

These are large mistakes caused by human errors being observed or recording data (incorrect recording of data values, using the wrong unit, miscalculation).

  • ▸ Caused by carelessness or lack of attention
  • ▸ Can be prevented by being careful and double-checking

Methods to Minimize Errors

  • Use precise instruments: Better tools give more accurate measurements
  • Calibrate equipment regularly: Ensure instruments are reading correctly
  • Take multiple measurements: Average several readings to reduce random errors
  • Control environmental factors: Minimize temperature changes, vibrations, etc.
  • Be careful and attentive: Avoid gross errors through careful observation
  • Use proper technique: Follow correct measurement procedures

Significant Figures and Rules for Rounding Off

Significant figures are the meaningful digits in a number that show how precise a measurement is. They include all the reliable digits we are certain about plus one estimated or uncertain digit. The more significant figures, the more precise a measurement is.

Rules for Identifying Figures

  • All non-zero digits are significant: 123.45 has 5 significant figures
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant: 1002 has 4 significant figures
  • Leading zeros are NOT significant: 0.0025 has 2 significant figures
  • Trailing zeros after decimal are significant: 2.500 has 4 significant figures
  • Trailing zeros without decimal may or may not be: 1500 is ambiguous (use scientific notation)

Percentage Error and Uncertainty

Percentage Error
Percentage error shows how far off a measured value is from the true or accepted value, expressed as a percentage. It tells you how accurate your measurement is. A smaller percentage error means your measurement is more accurate.
% Error = |Measured Value − True Value| / True Value × 100%

Example: You measure a book's mass as 1.2 kg, but the actual mass is 1.0 kg. The percentage error is: |1.2 − 1.0| / 1.0 × 100% = 20%

Uncertainty
Uncertainty is the range of doubt in a measurement. It tells you how sure (or unsure) you are about a measured value.

There are actually two ways to report uncertainty:

  • Absolute Uncertainty: The ± range of possible values (e.g., 5.3 ± 0.1 cm)
  • Relative/Percentage Uncertainty: Uncertainty as a percentage of the measurement
% Uncertainty = (Absolute Uncertainty / Measured Value) × 100%

Example: A measurement of 25.0 ± 0.5 cm has a percentage uncertainty of: (0.5 / 25.0) × 100% = 2%. The lower the percentage uncertainty, the more precise your measurement is. Devices with smaller divisions allow for lower uncertainties, making the measurements more precise.

Conclusion

Understanding accuracy, precision, and errors is essential for reliable scientific measurements. Accuracy tells us how close we are to the true value, while precision shows how consistent our measurements are. Errors (systematic, random, and gross) affect our measurements in different ways, but we can minimize them through careful technique and proper procedures. Significant figures, percentage error, and uncertainty help us communicate the quality and reliability of our measurements. By mastering these concepts, we can conduct better experiments and make more trustworthy scientific conclusions.
