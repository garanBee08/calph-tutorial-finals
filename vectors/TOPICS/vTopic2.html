Representation of Vectors

Vectors are fundamental in physics for describing quantities that have both magnitude and direction. Understanding how to represent and work with vectors is essential for solving problems in mechanics, electricity, and many other areas of physics.

Graphical Representation (Arrow Representation)

Visual Representation of Vectors
The most common way to represent a vector is by drawing it as an arrow. The arrow has three important features:
  • Length — represents the magnitude (size) of the vector
  • Direction — the way the arrow points shows the vector's direction
  • Arrowhead — indicates which way the vector is acting

Example: A velocity of 10 m/s to the east can be drawn as an arrow pointing right with a length proportional to 10 units.

Ways vectors are labeled as their start and end points (Tail/Head):

  • • Tail — Starting point of the vector
  • • Head — Endpoint (with the arrowhead)
  • • If a vector starts at point A and ends at point B, it can be written as: AB→ or A⃗B

Magnitude and Direction

Understanding Magnitude
The magnitude of a vector is its size or length. It tells us "how much" of the quantity we have. Magnitude is always a positive number (or zero).

Notation: The magnitude of vector A⃗ is written as |A⃗| or simply A.

Example: If a force vector has magnitude 50 N, we write |F⃗| = 50 N

How to Calculate Magnitude:

If you know the x and y components of a vector, you can find its magnitude using the Pythagorean theorem:

|A⃗| = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)

Example: If A⃗ has components Aₓ = 3 and Aᵧ = 4:

|A⃗| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5

Understanding Direction
The direction of a vector tells us which way it points. Direction can be described in several ways:
  • Using compass directions (north, south, east, west)
  • Using angles measured from a reference line (usually the positive x-axis)
  • Using bearing notation (e.g., 30° north of east)

Vector Components (x and y Directions)

Breaking Vectors into Components
Any vector can be broken down into two perpendicular components: one along the x-axis (horizontal) and one along the y-axis (vertical). This makes calculations much easier!

Components:

  • Aₓ — x-component (horizontal part)
  • Aᵧ — y-component (vertical part)

Finding Components from Magnitude and Angle:

If you know the magnitude |A⃗| and angle θ (measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis):

Aₓ = |A⃗| × cos(θ)
Aᵧ = |A⃗| × sin(θ)

Example: A force of 10 N at 30° above the horizontal:

Fₓ = 10 × cos(30°) = 10 × 0.866 = 8.66 N

Fᵧ = 10 × sin(30°) = 10 × 0.5 = 5 N

Finding Angle from Components:

If you know the components, you can find the direction angle:

θ = tan⁻¹(Aᵧ / Aₓ)

Example: If Aₓ = 3 and Aᵧ = 4:

θ = tan⁻¹(4/3) = tan⁻¹(1.333) ≈ 53.1°

Unit Vector Notation (î, ĵ)

What are Unit Vectors?
A unit vector is a vector with magnitude equal to 1. Unit vectors are used to indicate direction without worrying about magnitude.

Standard Unit Vectors:

  • î (i-hat) — points in the positive x-direction
  • ĵ (j-hat) — points in the positive y-direction
  • k̂ (k-hat) — points in the positive z-direction (for 3D)

Writing Vectors in Unit Vector Notation:

Any vector can be written as a combination of unit vectors:

A⃗ = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ

Example 1: A vector with x-component 3 and y-component 4:

A⃗ = 3î + 4ĵ

Example 2: A velocity of 5 m/s east and 2 m/s north:

v⃗ = 5î + 2ĵ m/s

Example 3: A force of 10 N west and 6 N south:

F⃗ = -10î - 6ĵ N (negative because west and south are negative directions)

Why Use Unit Vector Notation?
Unit vector notation makes vector operations much easier:
  • Adding and subtracting vectors becomes simple algebra
  • Clearly shows both magnitude and direction
  • Makes calculations more organized and less prone to errors
  • Easily extends to three dimensions with k̂

Vector Representation in Cartesian Coordinates

Cartesian Coordinate System
In the Cartesian (rectangular) coordinate system, vectors are represented using x and y coordinates (and z for 3D). This is one of the most practical ways to work with vectors.

Coordinate Notation:

A vector can be written as an ordered pair: A⃗ = (Aₓ, Aᵧ)

Or in component form: A⃗ = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ

Examples of Cartesian Representation:

Example 1: Position vector of a point at x = 5, y = 3

r⃗ = (5, 3) or r⃗ = 5î + 3ĵ

Example 2: Displacement from (1, 2) to (4, 6)

d⃗ = (4-1, 6-2) = (3, 4) or d⃗ = 3î + 4ĵ

Example 3: Velocity with components vₓ = -2 m/s, vᵧ = 5 m/s

v⃗ = (-2, 5) m/s or v⃗ = -2î + 5ĵ m/s

Converting Between Representations
From Polar (magnitude & angle) to Cartesian:
Aₓ = |A⃗| cos(θ)
Aᵧ = |A⃗| sin(θ)
From Cartesian to Polar:
|A⃗| = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)
θ = tan⁻¹(Aᵧ / Aₓ)

Conclusion

Understanding how to represent vectors is crucial for solving physics problems. Whether using graphical arrows, component notation, unit vectors, or Cartesian coordinates, each method has its advantages. Graphical representation helps visualize the problem, component notation simplifies calculations, unit vector notation provides a clear mathematical form, and Cartesian coordinates offer a systematic approach. Mastering these representations allows you to choose the best method for any given problem and convert between them as needed.
